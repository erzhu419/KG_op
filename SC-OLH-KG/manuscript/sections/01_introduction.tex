\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Many simulation decisions are rules before they are vectors.  A reserve rule
changes with forecast stress, an inventory rule changes over a planning
horizon, and a resource rule changes across ordered operating states.  A
computer may store such a rule as hundreds or thousands of numbers, but those
numbers are not unrelated knobs.  They are samples of one ordered profile.
Ignoring that fact can make the problem look much harder than the underlying
decision really is.

Chance constraints make the first simulations especially important.  If none
of them reaches a feasible region, a surrogate has little evidence about
where the safety boundary lies.  Historical simulations from related systems
can help, but only if the relevant policy shapes transfer.  A shape that was
safe in one system may be unsafe in another, and the cost of collecting and
verifying historical evidence may exceed the cost of the new search itself.
The useful question is therefore not simply whether history helps, but what
part of history can be reused, under what assumption, and at what cost.

We focus on one concrete use of history: choosing the \emph{initial design}
for a new chance-constrained ordered-profile problem.  We first construct a
public library of 64 constant, monotone, smooth, piecewise, and oscillatory
profiles without using outcomes.  Replicated source simulations then rank
these same profiles by objective and chance margin.  The best compromise
starts the design, and farthest-first selection adds profiles that cover a
common cosine coordinate.  The resulting ten policies are fixed and mapped to
the target grid before the first target outcome.  We call this a
\emph{source-scored structural initial design}.

The method keeps three jobs separate.  The initial design decides where a new
search begins.  Any declared optimizer may add target evaluations.  Finally,
a short ordered list of candidates is fixed and tested with fresh simulations;
if none passes, the procedure abstains.  Figure \ref{fig:method-overview}
shows the decision sequence.  We use the ordered profile and history to
choose among transparent candidate shapes,
and reserve fresh target outcomes for deployment verification.

The paper makes three contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item We give a finite, reproducible way to turn source simulations into ten
starting policies.  The library, coordinate, weights, tie breaks, and target
mapping are explicit, and the design uses no target outcome or hidden target
geometry.

\item We explain when the small design can work.  Grid refinement preserves
the profile coordinate; farthest-first controls finite-library cover; finite
source samples recover separated ranks, and selection gaps determine whether
source noise changes the design.  Structural cover reaches a target
safe region when transfer error fits inside its safety margin.  Fresh tests
control false deployment.  Lean 4 checks the original finite components, while
source-target alignment remains an empirical assumption.

\item We evaluate the resulting design in eight randomized regimes, a factorial
first-center/metric intervention, prospective confirmation on new source
families and public libraries, a same-source portfolio, equal-cost
experiments, postdecision fixed-first-center remainder controls, and a region-held-out
electricity study that does not establish a cost advantage.  A separate
public-battery development diagnostic is matched by constant-only search
and favors paid enumeration at the source-inclusive budget.  Source, target-search, and
verification calls are reported separately.  The evidence measures when source initialization helps under specified
task laws and when the archive cost remains unjustified.
\end{enumerate}

Across 160 independently seeded tasks and three grid resolutions, source
scoring certifies 47.3\% of task--resolution cells.  Supplementary comparisons
on these same tasks give 31.5\% for standardized outcome-free maximin and
37.1\% for the source-greedy portfolio.  The matched scaling corrects a
confound in the original outcome-free comparison, and the portfolio tests
whether geometric selection adds value beyond a simple use of source ranks.
The advantage varies by mechanism, with reversals in adverse regimes and no
established archive cost advantage in Energy.  On 240 new tasks the matched generic comparison remains positive,
but portfolio superiority is not reproduced.  Attribution identifies the
source-best first center as the main effective contribution.  The
fixed-first-center controls do not establish a certification advantage for
structural coverage over nine random remainder policies.  The
10,000-coordinate result concerns stability under grid refinement
of an ordered rule.
