\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the evidence supports}

Source outcomes improve certification relative to standardized generic
maximin on both the original mixture and new family instances.  The factorial
comparison identifies the source-best first center as the main effective
component.  Rank augmentation has little additional effect at the frozen
weights.  Structural coordinates improve certification over matched raw
profile distance, but geometric coverage is not uniformly preferable to
source-based portfolio selection: the prospective portfolio comparison does
not reproduce the original aggregate certification advantage and favors the
portfolio in coverage and penalized loss.  Which mechanism benefits also
changes with the family and public library.  In particular, coordinate
permutation contributes to the original gain but reverses in the new study.

Fixing the source-selected first policy also exposes the distinction between
design quality and deployment certification.  A random nine-policy remainder
produces similar certification to structural coverage.  The portfolio has
better feasible coverage but loses feasible candidates during noisy shortlist
selection.  Certification therefore measures the whole selection-and-testing
pipeline; it does not by itself establish a superior initial point set.

This interpretation differs from claiming a generic high-dimensional
optimizer.  The raw vector may contain $10^4$ coordinates, but the decision is
an ordered profile and the library has finite structural complexity.  A
controller may still require a fine lookup table, so profile-grid refinement
is operationally relevant.  It is not equivalent to adding $10^4$ unrelated
decision variables.  The proper benefit is invariance to representation
resolution under shared profile structure.

\subsection{What Energy teaches us}

Energy shows that a small aggregate certification gain need not repay the
source archive.  The corrected V5 model gives an unresolved region-balanced
gain over unstandardized maximin and about twice its calls per certificate.
Its finite seven-day service objective uses a common initial inventory,
shared hourly power and zero terminal salvage value.  This is a practical
boundary on archive use.  The public-battery diagnostic strengthens this
lesson: an outcome-free constant-policy search matches certification, and
paid target enumeration fits within the source-inclusive query budget.
For these development tasks, the archive is unnecessary to obtain the
reported certification outcome.

The practical implication is to compare source-scored and outcome-free
designs on held-out tasks with the same ordered semantics before committing
to archive reuse.  Energy alone cannot identify a universally preferable
scaling or a rule for detecting transfer failure before target outcomes.

The current method begins after this assessment: a source archive has already
been supplied, and the algorithm ranks profiles inside it.  It does not
retrieve related tasks from an unrestricted historical database.  Regime-matched
synthetic sources therefore identify the value of scoring a relevant archive,
whereas Energy shows an archive investment that does not pay off over the
observed deployments.  Source retrieval and its
own data-acquisition cost remain separate research and operational decisions.

\subsection{When the source archive pays for itself}

Ten target calls do not beat 394 target calls for a single target.  The source
archive becomes economically plausible only when it is reused.  Let
$C_0=N_0+V_0$ be the expected target-only calls per deployment and
$C_A=N_A+V_A$ the source-design calls excluding the archive.  The call-count
break-even number of deployments is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:break-even}
 M_{\rm break}=\left\lceil\frac{S}{C_0-C_A}\right\rceil,
 \qquad C_0>C_A.
\end{equation}
In the randomized primary experiment, the matched standardized control gives
a threshold of 15 targets using aggregate mean calls, and 15--16 targets when
each resolution is analyzed separately.  Both source-based designs pay the
same archive cost; lower verification effort favors the atlas over the
portfolio on average.  This arithmetic does not include differences in simulator setup,
wall-clock parallelism, or policy quality, and Energy shows that paying the
archive can still be inferior.  It is a planning threshold, not a dominance
theorem.

Table \ref{tab:outcome-adjusted-cost} also reports calls per certified
deployment, but that ratio is only a descriptive planning aid.  A complete
operational comparison would attach decision-maker costs to unsafe deployment,
abstention, and objective quality.  We report those components separately
rather than choose their relative value on the reader's behalf.

\subsection{Search and verification answer different questions}

Independent verification frequently costs an order of magnitude more than
target search because it answers a different question.  Search asks whether a
promising policy has been found.  Verification asks whether a fixed policy can
be deployed with a specified false-certification bound.  A target-only
optimizer making the same safety claim must also pay for fresh samples.

The all-success rule is valid but weak near the threshold, which explains why
feasible coverage exceeds certified deployment.  Future studies should fix a
more efficient exact or sequential test in advance and use block-aware
verification when temporal replications are not iid.

\subsection{Limitations}

The task law, library, and stress axes were created within the project.
The matched standardization and greedy-portfolio controls were added after
reviewing the original outcomes and reuse the same target seeds.  The fixed-first random-remainder and portfolio controls were likewise
proposed after observing confirmation results.  Their intervals describe
task variation within the existing families, not independent confirmation
or a guarantee of equivalence.  Positive intervals quantify sampling variation within the specified task law;
they do not remove development selection.  The separate prospective study
uses new family instances, source archives, public libraries and target tasks;
its intervals describe task variation conditional on those three fixed
families, rather than a population of all source libraries.
Ordered semantics are a strong prior.  Energy uses counterfactual storage
simulations on historical data from five regions, and the native transfer
study has only three historical families.
The public-battery controls were proposed after observing development
outcomes and use overlapping windows from one market.  Their certificates
refer to iid draws of window indices from a fixed uniform finite law, not
independent calendar observations or future deployment.  No sealed 2025
data are used.  Complete costs are ranked only for policies succeeding on
every window; failed full costs remain undefined.  Query accounting does
not establish wall-clock or monetary efficiency.
The archive is assumed to have been supplied; source retrieval is unsolved.
Three replications cannot order near ties, source aggregation can hide task
disagreement, and a finite library can miss the safe region entirely.  Regret
uses a finite evaluation library rather than the unknown continuous optimum,
and one functional-SCBO failure remains in the denominator.  The theory is
conditional on alignment, independence within each regime, and the declared
replication law.  Its finite geometric and sampling guarantees do not prove
superiority to either comparison design.  Finally, the same target seeds appear at all three grid
resolutions, so 480-cell totals are descriptive rather than 480 independent
tasks.
